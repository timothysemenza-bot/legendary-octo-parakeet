## Slide 1 - Cover
Target time: 0:45

Today I am presenting a Detroit Public Schools recompete that I led for RNA between November 2025 and January 2026. I selected this example because it shows not only how I managed a high-pressure pursuit, but how I turned that pursuit into something reusable for the business afterward.

My presentation will move through four points. First, I will explain the starting conditions and why the bid was high risk. Second, I will show how I led the team and structured the work. Third, I will show the measurable results that came out of the pursuit. Finally, I will explain how my current development plan builds directly on that impact.

## Slide 2 - Summary of Impact
Target time: 1:45

In roughly 20 working days, I took control of an unprepared 85 million dollar Detroit Public Schools recompete, coordinated about 15 stakeholders, reached 2 finalist presentations, and converted the work into reusable RNA school-bid infrastructure.

The point I want to emphasize here is the before-and-after change. At the beginning, this was a high-stakes pursuit with very little usable preparation, weak process discipline, and a great deal of dependence on a small number of people. By the end of the pursuit, we had not only submitted a credible bid and advanced to finalist presentations, but had also created reusable templates, graphics, narrative structure, and governance habits that were then used across later school bids.

So the impact was larger than one submission. The more meaningful outcome was that a reactive and inconsistent approach became a repeatable operating model for later RNA school-bid work.

## Slide 3 - The Situation
Target time: 2:30

The starting situation was difficult for three reasons. First, RNA entered the Detroit Public Schools recompete without a meaningful capture plan, even though this was incumbent work and the opportunity was too important to treat casually. Second, senior leaders had not engaged deeply enough with the RFP at the point the bid became active. Third, an external proposal consultant was added late, which meant we were still defining roles and working relationships while the pursuit was already underway.

The stakes were also unusually high. This incumbent work represented more than 25 percent of RNA's book of business, and the company wanted not just to defend the existing work but to make a much larger play. So this was not a routine production bid. It was a visible, time-compressed pursuit with material business significance.

Because of that, the central challenge was not simply writing fast. The real challenge was creating proposal governance while the proposal was already live. Before the team could compete credibly, someone had to establish structure, define responsibilities, create milestones, and give senior stakeholders a way to contribute productively. That became my leadership role in the pursuit.

## Slide 4 - Stakeholder Engagement and Communication
Target time: 2:30

This slide is really about leadership without formal authority. I was not leading from the C-suite, but I still had to align senior executives, the consultant, pricing, operations, IT, and site-level subject matter expertise around one coherent response.

To do that, I adapted my communication style to the people involved. I assigned work verbally during live calls because that was the fastest and most effective way to get engagement from senior stakeholders who were not proposal specialists. I then translated those decisions into structure through meeting summaries, action tracking, and a compliance matrix so the work remained visible and controlled.

When written input was vague, incomplete, or overly generic, I did not allow the process to stall. I moved people into short calls, interviewed them directly, and translated what they knew into usable proposal language. That was especially important with operational stakeholders whose expertise was strong, but whose input did not naturally arrive in proposal-ready form.

One example of that was the pricing and staffing coordination that Ann Couzins directly observed. I often had to bridge the gap between what pricing needed, what leadership meant, and what the proposal narrative needed to say. By doing that in real time, I kept pricing assumptions, staffing rationale, and written messaging aligned. In practice, that communication discipline was one of the reasons the bid kept moving.

## Slide 5 - Tasks
Target time: 2:15

I stabilized the pursuit through five main tasks. The first was to establish bid governance and the schedule. The second was to develop evaluator and win-theme strategy. The third was to elicit and structure subject matter expert input. The fourth was to draft and quality-control both the proposal and the finalist presentation content. The fifth was to lead review and submission readiness.

I think it is important to show the work in this way because it makes clear that this was not just a scramble that happened to succeed. It became manageable because I translated a chaotic situation into a small number of consistent leadership responsibilities.

Each task served a specific purpose. Governance replaced missing capture structure. Evaluator strategy kept the response focused on the customer rather than on internal opinion. SME elicitation turned operational knowledge into evidence. Drafting and quality control kept the written proposal and later presentation materials aligned. Review and submission readiness made sure the bid could still move forward even if something went wrong at the end.

The next slide shows how those tasks played out across the actual timeline of the pursuit.

## Slide 6 - Activities
Target time: 3:15

I have simplified the activities into five checkpoints because that is the clearest way to show how the bid actually moved.

The first checkpoint was plan. As soon as the RFP arrived, I used my Power Automate workflow to generate an initial schedule tied to review milestones. That let me produce a project plan quickly and establish cadence immediately rather than losing time to manual planning.

The second checkpoint was align. Early in the bid, I built the compliance matrix, researched the evaluation board, and entered kickoff already focused on the customer and the evaluators rather than letting the response become purely internally driven. That gave the team a shared frame for how we would position the bid.

The third checkpoint was build. This is where the drafting, consultant integration, and quality control accelerated. I used AI and automation to speed compliance extraction, transcript-based drafting, and early content assembly, but I remained responsible for the judgment, the message control, and the quality. I also used read-aloud quality checks in Word and recurring working sessions to keep the proposal coherent.

The fourth checkpoint was submit. I aligned executives for review, packaged the final files, and organized the SharePoint structure so the bid could still be submitted cleanly even if I became unavailable. That mattered because I did in fact become sick at the end of the cycle, and the structure I put in place allowed the handoff to work.

The fifth checkpoint was present. After submission, I carried the written narrative through into the finalist rounds by preparing slide messaging, speaker notes, likely questions, and response guidance. So the overall point of this slide is that recurring governance made a very short public-sector timeline executable.

## Slide 7 - Results
Target time: 3:30

I want to be careful here and stay within claims I can defend. I do not know the final award result, so I am not presenting impact in terms of a win claim. Instead, I am measuring the outcome through finalist progression, reuse, speed, and review effort.

First, the pursuit reached 2 finalist presentations. Given the starting point, that was a meaningful result because there was no assured advancement when the bid began.

Second, the Detroit work became a reusable base for later RNA school bids. About 12 later bids reused the assets that came out of this pursuit, and about 6 of those were active at one time in the immediate aftermath. That meant the Detroit effort did not end as a one-time response. It became the foundation for subsequent production.

Third, the time to reach an approximately 80 percent first compliant draft changed dramatically on later similar bids. Before Detroit, that work could take about 4 hours. After Detroit, when the bid pattern matched what we had already built, I could get to that draft state in about 5 minutes before tailoring.

Fourth, the executive review burden became lighter. Later bids usually needed only one executive review focused on pricing or solution differences, rather than repeated review of unfamiliar writing and structure. In other words, leadership became more comfortable because the messaging and format had become more standardized and more trusted.

So the strongest before-and-after statement I can make is this: before Detroit, RNA's school-bid process was ad hoc, low-trust, and review-heavy. After Detroit, it was more standardized, faster to launch, easier to review, and strong enough to support repeated reuse.
